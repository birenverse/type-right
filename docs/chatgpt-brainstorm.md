# Touch Typing Guide

## Learn to type without looking. Build accuracy. Then build speed.

Touch typing is the skill of typing without looking at the keyboard.

Instead of searching for each key with your eyes, you learn to associate keys with specific fingers and movements. With enough practice, those movements become automatic.

This guide gives you a practical path from **learning the keyboard** to **typing accurately and quickly**.

The approach is simple:

> **Learn → Train → Practice → Test → Improve**

---

# 1. Before You Start

There are a few rules that matter more than anything else in this guide.

### Don't look at the keyboard

This is the hardest rule for a beginner and one of the most important.

At first, looking down feels faster because you can immediately find the key you want. But the goal is to stop relying on your eyes altogether.

Keep your eyes on the screen and let your fingers learn the keyboard.

It is completely normal for your typing speed to become much slower when you first stop looking.

That's part of learning.

### Accuracy before speed

Don't chase WPM at the beginning.

If you repeatedly make mistakes while trying to type quickly, you are practicing those mistakes too.

A slower, accurate typist is building a much better foundation than a faster, inaccurate one.

> **First make the correct movement. Then make it faster.**

### Use the correct fingers

Don't press a key with whichever finger happens to be closest.

Each part of the keyboard has an assigned finger.

Using the correct fingers feels awkward at first. That awkwardness is temporary.

The objective is to turn those movements into habits.

### Practice regularly

Touch typing is a motor skill.

Reading about typing won't make you faster. Watching tutorials won't make you faster.

Your fingers need repetition.

Even short, consistent practice is useful.

# 2. Finger Placement

Before doing serious practice, learn where your fingers belong.

## The home row

The foundation of touch typing is the home row:

```text
A  S  D  F     J  K  L  ;
```

Your left hand rests on:

```text
A  S  D  F
```

Your right hand rests on:

```text
J  K  L  ;
```

Your thumbs rest around the spacebar.

The **F** and **J** keys normally have small physical bumps. These allow you to find the home position without looking at the keyboard.

Your index fingers should return to these positions after moving to other keys.

---

## Finger assignments

A simplified finger map looks like this:

```text
Left hand                         Right hand

Pinky   → Q A Z                  PINKY   → P ; /
Ring    → W S X                  RING    → O L .
Middle  → E D C                  MIDDLE  → I K ,
Index   → R F V T G B            INDEX   → Y H N U J M
```

The index fingers cover more keys than the other fingers.

The exact physical movement can vary slightly depending on the keyboard and typing technique, but the important thing is to establish consistent finger assignments and use them deliberately.

---

## Watch this before practicing

### Finger Placement Tutorial

[Watch the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vXsutlz0GIQ&t=20s)

Don't just watch it.

Put your hands on your keyboard and follow along.

Pay particular attention to:

- Home-row position
- Which finger belongs to each key
- How the index fingers move
- Returning your fingers toward the home row
- Using the spacebar correctly

Once you understand the basic position, begin practicing.

## Your first tool: TypingClub

[Open TypingClub](https://www.typingclub.com/)

TypingClub is the starting point for someone who is new to touch typing.

It teaches the keyboard progressively rather than throwing the entire keyboard at you at once.

You'll learn:

- Finger placement
- Individual keys
- Key combinations
- Words
- Sentences
- Capital letters
- Numbers and punctuation

### How to use TypingClub

Start from the beginning and follow the lessons.

Don't skip ahead just because you can already find some of the keys.

During the lessons:

**Use the instructed finger.**

**Don't look down.**

**Slow down when you start making mistakes.**

**Repeat lessons that don't feel comfortable yet.**

You do not need to achieve impressive WPM numbers here.

Your primary goal is:

> **Teach your fingers where the keys are.**

---

## When should you move forward?

Don't use a specific number of days as your rule.

Instead, move forward as the keyboard becomes increasingly familiar.

You should gradually reach a point where:

- You know roughly where the keys are without looking.
- You are using the intended fingers.
- You're no longer consciously searching for every key.
- Basic combinations feel increasingly automatic.

You won't be perfect.

The next stage will continue developing these skills.

# 4. Train Your Fingers with Keybr

## Why Keybr looks weird

[Open Keybr](https://www.keybr.com/)

This is the part of the process that can confuse beginners.

You may see text like:

```text
fjdk auei rocl dksf
jrei foad lskd...
```

and think:

> **"What am I supposed to be typing? This isn't even English."**

That's intentional.

**Keybr is fundamentally different from a normal typing test.**

It isn't primarily testing how quickly you can type familiar words.

It is training your keyboard movements.

---

## Keybr is not trying to teach you vocabulary

Consider a familiar word:

```text
the
```

If you type it repeatedly, your brain can recognize the whole word as a familiar pattern.

You aren't necessarily thinking separately about:

```text
T → H → E
```

You recognize the word and your fingers execute a familiar movement.

That's useful in real typing.

But it can also hide weaknesses.

You might be very comfortable with common words while still being slow or hesitant with particular letters and key combinations.

Keybr works differently.

---

## What Keybr is actually training

Keybr focuses on individual characters and the movements required to type them.

The objective is to gradually turn:

```text
"I need to find this key."
```

into:

```text
I know where this key is.
```

and eventually:

```text
I don't need to think about the key at all.
```

That is the kind of automaticity you want from touch typing.

---

## Why gibberish can be useful

Imagine you type:

```text
because because because because
```

You become familiar with the word itself.

Now imagine you're given:

```text
beacsu becsaue bacuse...
```

You can't rely on recognizing a familiar word.

You have to execute the individual keystrokes.

That's why Keybr's generated text can feel strange.

> **Keybr deliberately reduces your ability to rely on word familiarity so that your fingers do more of the work.**

This is not a typing game where you're supposed to understand the sentence.

Think of the generated text as a sequence of **keyboard movements to practice**.

---

## Keybr focuses on your weaknesses

Another important feature is that Keybr can adapt its exercises based on your performance.

Some characters may appear more often because you're slower or less accurate with them.

So don't assume that repeated letters mean something is broken.

The exercise is trying to give you more practice where you need it.

Conceptually:

```text
Your typing
     ↓
Identify weaker characters
     ↓
Practice them more
     ↓
Improve the movement
     ↓
Reduce the weakness
```

This makes Keybr particularly useful after you've learned the basic keyboard layout.

---

## How to use Keybr

### Don't worry about whether the text makes sense

You aren't reading it.

You're training your fingers.

### Don't memorize the generated text

The goal isn't to remember a sequence.

The goal is to strengthen the underlying movements.

### Use the correct fingers

This matters enormously.

If you use the wrong finger every time, you may become faster at the wrong technique.

### Don't race

Let accuracy and smoothness lead the session.

### Expect it to feel slower

Keybr can feel considerably slower than normal-word typing.

That's not necessarily a sign that you're getting worse.

You're doing a different kind of exercise.

---

## Keybr vs. Monkeytype

These two tools can look similar because both involve typing on a screen, but they serve different purposes.

| | Keybr | Monkeytype |
|---|---|---|
| Primary purpose | Train keyboard movements | Practice overall typing |
| Text | Generated/artificial combinations | Mostly words and readable text |
| Main focus | Characters and weak areas | Speed, accuracy, rhythm, consistency |
| Best use | Strengthening the foundation | Developing overall typing ability |
| Typical experience | Strange at first | Familiar and game-like |

Think of them this way:

> **Keybr trains your fingers. Monkeytype trains your overall typing performance.**

You don't need to choose one.

They complement each other.

## What makes someone faster?

Typing speed is not simply about moving your fingers harder or faster.

A lot of speed comes from eliminating hesitation.

Slow typing often looks like:

```text
See word
   ↓
Think about first letter
   ↓
Find key
   ↓
Press
   ↓
Find next key
   ↓
Pause
```

As skill develops, the process becomes closer to:

```text
See word
   ↓
Recognize pattern
   ↓
Automatic finger movement
```

The less conscious searching you have to do, the faster and smoother you become.

# 6. Understanding WPM and Accuracy

## What is WPM?

**WPM** means **Words Per Minute**.

Typing tests use a standardized definition of a "word" so that results can be compared between tests.

For example:

> 40 WPM means your measured typing rate is approximately 40 standardized words per minute.

WPM is useful, but it is only one measurement.

---

## What is accuracy?

Accuracy tells you how much of what you typed was correct.

For example:

```text
40 WPM
98% accuracy
```

is generally a much better result than:

```text
50 WPM
80% accuracy
```

because the second typist is making many more mistakes.

A useful way to think about it is:

> **Speed tells you how fast you're going. Accuracy tells you how cleanly you're getting there.**

Both matter.

---

## Raw WPM

You may also encounter **Raw WPM**, especially in Monkeytype.

Raw WPM represents your typing rate before mistakes are accounted for in the same way as the displayed WPM.

This helps explain why you might see something like:

```text
Raw:      48 WPM
Accuracy: 94%
WPM:      45 WPM
```

The exact calculations depend on the typing test, but conceptually:

> **Raw WPM = how fast you typed.**

> **Displayed WPM = a speed measure that takes errors into account.**

---

## Why does my WPM keep changing?

This is normal.

Your result can change depending on:

- Which words appear
- How familiar those words are
- How many mistakes you make
- Test duration
- Concentration
- Fatigue
- Your typing rhythm

A single test doesn't tell you much.

Look at your performance over many sessions.

# 8. How to Actually Increase Your Typing Speed

Once you know touch typing, improvement comes from gradually making the process more automatic.

## 1. Stop looking at the keyboard

You cannot develop strong touch-typing habits while constantly depending on your eyes.

Make the screen your visual reference.

---

## 2. Improve accuracy

If you're making many mistakes, slow down.

Your current speed may simply be beyond what your fingers can reliably handle.

---

## 3. Eliminate hesitation

Notice where you pause.

Are certain letters slow?

Are particular combinations uncomfortable?

Do numbers or punctuation interrupt your rhythm?

These are useful weaknesses to work on.

---

## 4. Strengthen weak keys

Use Keybr to identify and practice areas that are holding you back.

---

## 5. Develop rhythm

Good typing often feels smooth rather than frantic.

Compare:

```text
type → stop → type → stop → type
```

with:

```text
type type type type type
```

You want the second feeling.

Smoothness generally comes before very high speed.

---

## 6. Gradually challenge yourself

Once your normal typing feels comfortable, occasionally push beyond your comfortable speed.

It is useful to experience faster movement.

But don't spend every practice session trying to achieve a personal record.

---

# 9. Don't Confuse Typing Tests with Real Typing

A typing test is useful, but it isn't the final goal.

Eventually you need to type things that you actually care about:

- Emails
- Messages
- Documents
- Essays
- Notes
- Searches
- Programming code

Real-world typing introduces things that many simple tests don't fully represent:

- Capitalization
- Punctuation
- Numbers
- Symbols
- Unpredictable words
- Long passages

A person who can type 80 WPM on a simple test but struggles to write a normal paragraph comfortably still has room to improve.

> **The real goal is effortless everyday typing, not just a high test score.**

# 10. Common Problems

## "I keep looking at the keyboard."

Slow down and force yourself to keep your eyes on the screen.

It will feel worse before it feels better.

---

## "I'm extremely slow."

That's normal when you're learning.

Don't respond by abandoning proper finger placement.

Give your fingers time to learn.

---

## "My accuracy is terrible."

You're probably typing faster than your current skill supports.

Slow down until you can type more reliably.

Then gradually increase speed.

---

## "I keep using the wrong finger."

Don't ignore it.

Slow down and consciously use the intended finger until the correct movement becomes habitual.

---

## "Keybr is much slower than Monkeytype."

That's expected.

They are doing different things.

Keybr deliberately gives you unfamiliar character combinations and emphasizes weaknesses, so it can feel much harder than normal-word typing.

Don't compare the two WPM numbers directly.

---

## "I'm stuck at the same WPM."

Don't just take hundreds of more speed tests.

Look for the cause.

You may have:

- Weak characters
- Frequent mistakes
- Hesitation
- Poor rhythm
- Tension
- Reliance on a few fingers
- Difficulty with punctuation or numbers

Identify the bottleneck and train that specific weakness.

---

## "I'm fast in typing tests but slow when writing normally."

Practice real text.

Typing tests are a useful measurement, but real typing is a separate part of the skill.

---

## "My hands feel tense."

Don't try to type through pain or significant discomfort.

Reduce your speed.

Relax your fingers.

Avoid pressing keys harder than necessary.

Make your setup and posture comfortable.

---

# 11. Common Mistakes to Avoid

### Looking at the keyboard

You're training yourself to depend on visual searching.

### Using only a few fingers

It can work, but it limits the development of proper touch typing.

### Ignoring finger assignments

You're building inconsistent habits.

### Chasing WPM

Speed without control produces sloppy typing.

### Practicing only tests

Tests measure performance; they don't necessarily fix your weaknesses.

### Ignoring punctuation and numbers

Real-world typing needs more than lowercase words.

### Practicing only familiar words

Familiarity can hide weaknesses in individual key movements.

### Trying to force speed

Speed should be developed gradually.

# 12. The Complete Learning Path

The entire approach can be summarized as:

```text
Learn finger placement
          ↓
TypingClub
          ↓
Learn the keyboard
          ↓
Keybr
          ↓
Strengthen weak characters
          ↓
Monkeytype
          ↓
Develop speed, rhythm & consistency
          ↓
10FastFingers
          ↓
Measure your performance
          ↓
Real-world typing
          ↓
Continue improving
```

You don't have to permanently "graduate" from one tool.

You can move between them depending on what you're trying to improve.

For example:

> Weak letter → Keybr

> Need general practice → Monkeytype

> Need to measure progress → 10FastFingers

> Forgot proper technique → TypingClub

# 13. The Skill Progression

Typing ability generally develops in this direction:

```text
Keyboard awareness
        ↓
Correct finger placement
        ↓
Accuracy
        ↓
Muscle memory
        ↓
Smoothness
        ↓
Consistency
        ↓
Speed
        ↓
Effortless real-world typing
```

Don't try to skip the early stages.

High typing speed is usually the result of doing the earlier things well.
